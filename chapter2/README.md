# Chapter 2: The Principle of the Invariance of the Speed of Light

### 1. Continuing from Chapter 1

In Chapter 1, we discussed the law of conservation of momentum. It tells us that when a system experiences no external force, its total momentum remains constant. Within the GULP framework, this means that a system, amid change, still retains an invariant constraint—the Zero-Bug Degree does not mean that a system never changes, but rather that it maintains its own continuity and stability throughout the process of change.

But conservation of momentum is only the first law. It answers "what remains unchanged." It does not answer another key question: how does change itself propagate? How does a localized event affect other parts of the system?

To answer this question, we need to bring in the second law.

### 2. The Principle of the Invariance of the Speed of Light

The principle of the invariance of the speed of light states: the speed of light in a vacuum is constant in all inertial reference frames, independent of the relative motion of the light source and the observer.

This constant value is precisely equal to:

> c = 299,792,458 m/s

approximately three hundred thousand kilometers per second.

This principle contains two layers of meaning. First, the speed of light is a constant that does not change with the reference frame. You measure the speed of light standing on the ground, and the result is c. You measure it aboard a spaceship traveling at near-light speed, and the result is still c. You race toward the light source, or travel away from it, and the speed of light remains c. Second, the speed of light is independent of the motion of the light source. If the source is stationary, the light it emits travels at c. If the source moves at near-light speed, the light it emits still travels at c. Once light leaves its source, it does not inherit the source's velocity of motion, but propagates according to its own laws.

This is completely different from everyday experience. If you throw a ball forward on a high-speed train, the ball's speed relative to the ground equals the train's speed plus the speed at which you threw it. This is classical velocity addition. But if you shine a flashlight forward on a high-speed train, the speed of light relative to the ground is not the speed of light plus the train's speed—it is still the speed of light. The classical velocity addition formula no longer applies when it comes to light—relativity has its own more precise velocity addition formula, which ensures that no combined velocity ever exceeds the speed of light.

### 3. The Michelson-Morley Experiment

The invariance of the speed of light is not an assumption pulled from thin air, but a physical principle established and continuously verified on the basis of extensive experimentation.

In 1887, Michelson and Morley designed an interferometer to measure the effect of the Earth's motion through the "ether" on the speed of light. The prevailing view in the physics community at the time was that light propagated through a medium called "ether," just as sound travels through air. If the ether existed, then as the Earth moved, it should—like a ship sailing through water—detect differences in the speed of light coming from different directions: light traveling along the direction of Earth's motion should be faster, while light traveling against it should be slower.

They used a half-silvered mirror to split a beam of light into two beams, one traveling along the direction of Earth's motion and one perpendicular to it. After being reflected, the two beams recombined to produce interference fringes. If the speed of light differed along the two paths, the interference fringes should shift. To improve precision, they mounted the entire apparatus on a mercury pool and rotated it, taking repeated measurements during the day and at night, across different seasons.

The result was null.

The interference fringes remained unchanged. No matter how they rotated the apparatus, no matter when they measured, the speed of light along both paths was exactly the same. The experiment found no evidence for the motion effect predicted by the classical ether theory, making it a crucial piece of evidence against that theory.

This experiment is one of the most famous "failed experiments" in the history of physics—it did not detect the expected result, but its null result was more significant than any positive result could have been. It directly shook the absolute space and time framework of Newtonian mechanics and gave birth to special relativity. Moreover, it is remarkably robust; to this day, you can replicate it in any precision optics laboratory.

### 4. The Physical Meaning of the Speed of Light

The invariance of the speed of light is not an isolated law. It directly led to a fundamental restructuring of the fabric of spacetime.

In Newtonian mechanics, time and space are independent and absolute. Time flows uniformly, and space remains fixed. But the invariance of the speed of light forcibly requires that time and space be linked. If the speed of light is invariant in all reference frames, then time and space in different reference frames must coordinate with each other—like a ruler and a clock that must adjust together so that everyone measures the same speed of light.

This is the core of special relativity. Simultaneity is relative. Time dilates. Length contracts. These effects are not mathematical games; they have all been repeatedly verified in experiments. GPS satellites must calibrate for relativistic effects, otherwise positioning errors would accumulate to ten kilometers per day. Unstable particles in particle accelerators have their lifetimes significantly extended by moving at speeds close to that of light, precisely matching relativistic predictions.

The speed of light not only describes how fast light travels; it also determines the causal structure of spacetime. No signal, no energy, no information can be transmitted faster than the speed of light. This is not a technological limitation; it is a logical one. If a signal could travel faster than light, it would mean that in some reference frames, the effect would appear before the cause. Causality would be inverted. Logic would collapse.

From an evolutionary perspective, the speed of light defines the maximum speed at which causal chains can extend through the universe. The formation of any complex structure must be built upon the foundation of finite-speed information propagation. The formation of stars, the progress of chemical reactions, the evolution of life, and even the emergence of consciousness all depend on the gradual accumulation of information across space and time, rather than happening instantaneously.

### 5. Invariance of the Speed of Light and the Evolutionary Curve

The invariance of the speed of light imposes a mandatory constraint on GULP's evolutionary curve.

The evolutionary curve requires that changes in a system's state remain continuous along the time axis. The invariance of the speed of light provides a physical upper limit for this continuity: the speed of information transfer between any two moments of state change cannot exceed the speed of light. If a change at point A in the system is to affect point B, that influence must propagate at a speed no greater than the speed of light.

This means the evolutionary curve is not only continuous but also causally continuous. The physical influence at time t₁ propagates at a finite speed to time t₂, and there exists no superluminal action that could be used to transmit information.

One clarification is needed here: the inflation of the early universe involved the exponential expansion of space itself, a process that cannot be directly analogized to the motion of ordinary matter through space. The dynamical mechanism of the inflationary phase remains an open question, awaiting deeper theoretical explanation.

### 6. Invariance of the Speed of Light and the Zero-Bug Degree

The invariance of the speed of light also provides an important physical condition for GULP's Zero-Bug Degree.

The Zero-Bug Degree requires that a system maintain internal coherence amid change. Within the GULP framework, the invariance of the speed of light supplies a necessary condition for the Zero-Bug Degree: change must propagate at a finite speed in order for the system to possess a process of response and adjustment. Because signal propagation has an upper limit, disturbances within the system do not instantly spread throughout the whole. A local disturbance has a physical influence whose propagation speed cannot exceed the speed of light. The system has reaction time, can adjust step by step, absorb disturbances layer by layer, and resolve problems locally without instantly suffering global collapse.

If influences in the universe could propagate instantaneously—similar to the instantaneous action in Newton's absolute space and time—then there would be no hierarchical evolutionary process between local disturbances and the global state, and system stability would face an entirely different kind of challenge. A local inconsistency could act directly upon the entire system without any buffer, and the causal adjustment process on which the Zero-Bug Degree depends would lose an important physical foundation.

The invariance of the speed of light gives disturbances a propagation time and gives the system room to adjust. It does not prove the Zero-Bug Degree, but it provides an indispensable physical prerequisite for its realization.

Conservation of momentum ensures that there exists an invariant constraint in the evolutionary process. The invariance of the speed of light ensures the integrity of causal structure and provides a time window for systemic adjustment.

But one question still hangs in the air: evolutionary dynamics. What drives the change of a system? The two laws provide constraints, but they do not provide the answer. We will leave this question for the next chapter.
