# Grand Unified Logic Chain: The Evolution of the Universe

## Preface

The evolution of the universe. We begin with the origin of the universe.

There are several mainstream versions. The main versions regarding the origin of the universe are roughly as follows:

**1. The Big Bang Theory (Standard Cosmological Model)**

The universe began expanding from an extremely hot and dense initial state, went through inflation, primordial nucleosynthesis, and photon decoupling, eventually forming galaxies and stars. This is the mainstream model with the most observational evidence, but unresolved problems remain regarding singularity physics and the inflation mechanism.

**2. The Cyclic Universe Model**

The universe undergoes infinite cycles of expansion and contraction, with each Big Bang being a bounce rather than an absolute beginning. This avoids the singularity problem but lacks direct observational evidence.

**3. Quantum Gravity Models (such as the Hartle-Hawking No-Boundary Proposal)**

In the framework of quantum gravity, the universe has no boundary, and time manifests as a spatial dimension in the early stage. The singularity is eliminated by quantum effects, and the universe is self-contained, requiring no "starting point."

**4. The Multiverse Model (Inflationary Multiverse)**

Inflation not only produced our observable universe, but also produced countless disconnected universes on a larger scale. Our universe is just one bubble among them.

**5. String Theory / Brane World Model**

The universe originated from the collision of two branes in higher-dimensional space, with the Big Bang being the result of this collision. This model attempts to unify gravity with quantum mechanics but currently cannot be experimentally verified.

GULP does not ask how the universe began. GULP asks: can the evolution of the universe be reconstructed? If so, from which point? If not, why not?

The problem of particle speed further exposes the fractures in the evolutionary curve. If the singularity explosion was isotropic, particles scattered in all directions from the center. What speed would a particle need to reach the edge of today's observable universe from the center? The radius of the universe is about 46 billion light-years, while the age of the universe is only 13.8 billion years. For a proton to traverse 46 billion light-years in 13.8 billion years, its average speed would have to be more than three times the speed of light. But a proton has mass, and relativity forbids any massive particle from reaching the speed of light, let alone exceeding it.

Even if we step back and assume the proton flies at near light speed, its kinetic energy would be absurdly large. A proton near light speed, with a mass of only one trillion-trillionth of a gram, would carry kinetic energy equivalent to a fully loaded truck colliding head-on at highway speed, with the impact point being only the size of an atomic nucleus. And this is not just one proton — it is countless protons. For all the matter in the universe to disperse at such speeds, the initial energy required is simply unimaginable.

Even more troubling is the problem of deceleration. How did these high-speed particles later slow down? The cosmic matter we observe today moves at speeds far below light speed — galaxies rotate slowly, nebulae contract gently under gravity. If particles had been flying since the singularity explosion, we should today be bombarded from all directions by near-light-speed particles. But we see no such thing. Where did those initial high-speed particles go? What mechanism braked them? The evolutionary curve, in the transition from high-speed particles to slow-moving matter, lacks continuous observational data to fill the gap.

Supporters of the Big Bang theory may respond to these particle speed problems by saying: it is not that particles are flying through space, but that space itself is expanding. Particles are being carried along by space, not running on their own. Therefore, superluminal speed is not required.

This explanation does dodge one difficulty, but it brings another. Space itself can expand faster than light — general relativity permits the expansion speed of space itself to exceed light speed, because that is spacetime structure stretching, not matter moving through space. So particles do not need to fly on their own; space carries them along. The speed problem is shifted onto space.

But the bill has not really been settled. The speed of spatial expansion is not a constant — incredibly fast during inflation, suddenly slowing after inflation ends, and then mysteriously accelerating again billions of years later. Three changes of rate, each a sudden turn. What drove the start of inflation? What made it stop? And what made the universe accelerate again billions of years later? Cosmology has given these three turns names — inflaton, dark energy, cosmological constant — but names are not explanations, only labels. Evolutionary dynamics demands a driving force for every turn, and the Big Bang theory offers three labels, not driving forces.

Shifting the speed problem onto spatial expansion is just moving the bill from the left hand to the right hand. Why does space speed up and slow down? Who controls the start and stop of inflation? Why is the cosmological constant exactly the value it is now? These questions are, at root, the same kind of problem as the original speed problem: the absence of evolutionary dynamics. The Big Bang theory describes the curve of spatial expansion's speed changes, but it offers no source of force to drive those changes. Motion with variable speed and no engine does not hold in the GULP framework either.

Now consider the deceleration problem once more. Even if spatial expansion explains why particles do not need to fly on their own, the deceleration problem still exists. Particles in expanding space have their own peculiar velocities. The early universe was hot and dense, with photons and charged particles colliding frequently and radiation pressure at its maximum. As space expanded and the temperature dropped, collision frequencies decreased, and the peculiar velocities of particles should have gradually diminished. But the details of this deceleration process — the rate, the time nodes, the deceleration curves of different particles — lack continuous observational data. We know that particles are very slow today, and we know they may have been very fast in the early universe, but how the transition happened in between — there are not enough logical nodes to fill the gap. The evolutionary curve is still broken here.
