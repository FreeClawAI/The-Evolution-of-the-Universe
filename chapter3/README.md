# Chapter 3: The Principle of Relativity

## 1. Two Foundational Principles: Continuity and Observational Consistency

Before entering the principle of relativity, we need to first understand several important concepts that underpin modern physics: inertia, symmetry, consistency, and Noether's theorem.

These concepts may seem to belong to different domains, but they are all actually answering the same question—**why can the universe operate stably, rather than falling into patternless change?**

---

### 1.1 Inertia: Continuity Amid Change

Newton's first law tells us: if an object is not acted upon by an external force, it will remain at rest or continue in uniform rectilinear motion. This property is called inertia.

The core meaning of inertia is: **an object does not change its state of motion without cause.** For example, if a spaceship in deep space shuts off its engines and is not acted upon by other objects, it will not suddenly stop, nor will it suddenly change direction—it will continue moving forward at its original speed.

At the classical scale, this shows that macroscopic change in the universe is not random jumping, but unfolds through continuous dynamical processes. To go from one state to another requires a continuous process in between.

The law of conservation of momentum discussed in Chapter 1 is a further embodiment of this continuity. A particle can collide. A star can explode. A galaxy can merge. On the surface, objects in the universe are constantly changing, but in these processes of change, there are always certain quantities that remain invariant. Conservation of momentum tells us: in a closed system, total momentum does not vanish because of change. Particles can change their state of motion, can collide, can reorganize, but the entire process must satisfy conservation of momentum.

Thus, evolution in the universe is not simple state substitution, but occurs continuously under constraints.

From the GULP perspective, this corresponds to continuity in the evolutionary process. A system that can truly persist over the long term must maintain its core structure throughout the process of change. This is also the first layer of the physical foundation of the Zero-Bug Degree: **a system can change, but it cannot lose the logic of its own existence through change.**

---

### 1.2 Symmetry: Laws Do Not Depend on Special Conditions

Inertia answers "why an object maintains its state of motion," while symmetry answers a different question: **why do the laws of physics not change when the mode of description changes?**

What is symmetry? Simply put, if you change the way you observe and the laws of physics still hold, then that law possesses symmetry. For example, perform an experiment on Earth today, then move the same experiment to the Moon tomorrow—as long as conditions are the same, the laws still hold. This is called spatial translation symmetry. Perform an experiment today, then perform the same experiment a hundred years later—the laws still hold. This is called time translation symmetry.

One must be careful here: symmetry does not mean everything looks the same; it means that **after changing the mode of description, the underlying laws that support phenomena do not change.** The universe can have different positions, different times, different observational angles, but the laws themselves remain stable.

---

### 1.3 Consistency: Different Observers Share the Same Set of Physical Laws

Symmetry leads to another, even more important question: **do different observers see a universe that follows the same set of rules?**

Suppose there are two observers in the universe. Xiao Ming stands on Earth. Xiao Hong sits in a spaceship flying at high speed. They observe the same physical process. If Xiao Ming sees one set of laws and Xiao Hong, due to her different state of motion, sees a completely different set of laws, then the universe has no unified description. Each observer would possess a universe belonging only to themselves. This means that the laws of physics would no longer be laws of the universe itself, but merely the special rules of a particular observer.

But if the universe truly has a unified structure, then although the phenomena observed by different observers may differ, the underlying laws describing those phenomena must remain consistent. This is the requirement of consistency in physics. In other words: different observers share the same set of physical laws.

The principle of relativity is built precisely upon this idea. It tells us: **no observer holds a special status; no reference frame is the absolute center of the universe.**

---

### 1.4 Noether's Theorem: Symmetry Produces Conservation Laws

In 1918, the mathematician Emmy Noether proposed her famous theorem. It reveals an extremely profound connection: **every continuous symmetry corresponds to a conservation law.**

- Spatial translation symmetry → conservation of momentum. The laws of the universe do not change with position, so momentum must be conserved.
- Time translation symmetry → conservation of energy. The laws today and the laws tomorrow are the same, so energy must be conserved.
- Rotational symmetry → conservation of angular momentum. The laws do not change with direction, so angular momentum must be conserved.

Within the existing framework of physical theory, conservation laws are not rules artificially imposed by anyone; they arise from deeper symmetrical structures.

---

### 1.5 The Relationship Among the Four Concepts

To avoid confusion, their respective roles can be sorted out as follows:

- **Inertia** answers: why does an object not change its state of motion without cause?—it describes the continuity of states of motion.
- **Symmetry** answers: why do laws still hold after changing the mode of observation?—it describes the invariance of physical laws.
- **Consistency** answers: why do different observers describe the same universe?—it describes the unity across different observational perspectives.
- **Noether's Theorem** answers: why does symmetry produce conserved quantities?—it builds the bridge between symmetrical structures and conservation laws.

These four concepts together form an important foundation of modern physics: inertia ensures that change has continuity, conservation laws ensure that change has constraints, symmetry ensures that laws have stability, and consistency ensures that different observers can share the same universe.

Only on this foundation can we formally enter the principle of relativity.

## 2. A New Question

In Chapter 1, we discussed the law of conservation of momentum. It tells us: change in the universe does not happen without rules. A system can collide, split, reorganize, but there exist invariant constraints in the process of change. The system does not lose its continuity through change. This provides the foundation of continuity for understanding cosmic evolution.

In Chapter 2, we discussed the principle of the invariance of the speed of light. It tells us: change in the universe does not propagate instantaneously. All information, energy, and influence have a maximum speed of propagation—the speed of light. This provides a limit on the causal structure of the universe.

But now, a new question emerges. If every observer in the universe is in a different state of motion, then **do different observers see the same universe?**

Xiao Ming stands on Earth. Xiao Hong sits in a spaceship flying at high speed. They simultaneously observe an event. Will the time, distance, and speed that Xiao Ming sees be exactly the same as the time, distance, and speed that Xiao Hong sees? If they are not the same, then what is the real universe? Does the universe have an absolutely correct observational perspective?

This question leads to the principle of relativity.

---

## 3. Newton's Absolute Universe

Before the emergence of relativity, humanity understood the world in this way: space is a fixed grand stage, and time is a uniformly flowing river. No matter who stands where, no matter how they move, space is the same space, and time is the same time. This is the Newtonian view of absolute space and time.

In this world, all that is needed between different observers is a simple conversion of velocity.

A high-speed train moves forward at 300 kilometers per hour. You throw a ball forward on the train at a speed of 20 kilometers per hour. The speed of the ball as seen by a person on the ground is: 300 + 20 = 320 kilometers per hour. This is classical velocity addition.

In the Newtonian universe, velocity can increase without limit, time never changes, and space never changes. The entire universe possesses an absolute background. Everything is perfectly intuitive—until light appears.

---

## 4. The Challenge Posed by Light

According to classical velocity addition, if a spaceship moves at near-light speed and a person aboard turns on a flashlight, an observer on the ground should see: the speed of light equals the spaceship's speed plus the speed of light itself. In other words, light should exceed the speed of light.

But experiments tell us: no. No matter how the light source moves, no matter how the observer moves, the measured speed of light is always c ≈ 299,792,458 meters per second.

In 1887, Michelson and Morley attempted to measure the effect of Earth's motion on the speed of light using an interference experiment. The results did not show the expected variation in the speed of light. Over the following century, a large number of experiments further confirmed: **the speed of light does not change due to changes in the observer's state of motion.**

This raises a profound question: if space and time are completely fixed, the invariance of the speed of light cannot hold. Yet experiments repeatedly tell us that the speed of light must remain invariant.

Something deeply significant happens here. When classical velocity addition demands a variable speed of light, while experiment demands an invariant speed of light, the universe does not choose to abandon one of them; instead, it transforms a deeper layer of structure to accommodate the new constraint. Absolute space and absolute time must be reorganized into relative spacetime, and the Galilean transformation must be replaced by the Lorentz transformation. This is precisely the mode of evolution described by the GULP framework: when new constraint conditions emerge, the system achieves compatibility through a reorganization of its underlying structure, rather than evading difficulty by discarding established principles.

Then, something must change.

---

## 5. What Must the Universe Change?

In classical intuition, speed equals the ratio of the change in distance to the change in time. If the speed must remain invariant, then distance and time must coordinate their changes.

Suppose two observers measure the same process of light propagation. One observer measures the light propagation distance as s and the elapsed time as t, satisfying s/t = c. The other observer measures the distance as s' and the time as t', likewise satisfying s'/t' = c.

This means: **between different observers, space and time cannot be completely independent.** They must be linked together. Space and time are not two completely separate backgrounds, but together form a single whole—spacetime.

---

## 6. The Principle of Relativity

Einstein further proposed: **the laws of physics are the same in all inertial reference frames.**

What is an inertial reference frame? Simply put, it is the environment of an observer who is not subject to significant external forces and remains at rest or in uniform rectilinear motion. For example, a car parked on the ground, a high-speed train moving at constant velocity, a spaceship flying smoothly.

In these reference frames, the laws of physics do not change because the observer is different. Within the scope of inertial reference frames, no observer holds a special status. Earth is not the center of the universe. The Sun is not the center of the universe. No observer can say: "My time is the true time. My space is the true space." **The universe has no absolute observer.**

---

## 7. A Simple Experiment

Imagine a sealed spaceship flying at constant velocity through space. The people inside perform all kinds of experiments: throwing a ball, measuring the speed of light, observing the motion of objects. Without a window, they cannot determine through any internal experiment whether they are at rest or in uniform motion. Because all the laws of physics inside the spaceship are exactly the same.

However, if the spaceship begins to accelerate, the situation is different. Acceleration means a change in the state of motion, and additional physical effects will arise.

This illustrates: **uniform motion itself has no absolute meaning; only motion relative to other objects has meaning.**

---

## 8. The Principle of Relativity and Cosmic Evolution

The principle of relativity tells us: the laws of the universe cannot depend on a single special observational perspective. Different observers can have different positions, different states of motion, and even different measurement results, but the underlying laws must remain consistent.

This aligns with the requirement of stability in cosmic evolution. In a truly stable system, external manifestations can change, observational angles can change, but the underlying logic that supports the system's operation cannot change arbitrarily. It is like the same sports match: different spectators sit in different seats and see different perspectives, but the rules of the match do not change.

---

## 9. From the Principle of Relativity to the Lorentz Transformation

At this point, we have arrived at two important conditions. First, the laws of physics observed by all inertial observers must be the same. Second, the speed of light measured by all inertial observers must be the same.

But the combination of these two conditions immediately gives rise to a new question: if the time and distance measured by different observers are not the same, then how exactly do time and space transform between them? Newton's Galilean transformation can no longer satisfy the invariance of the speed of light. The universe requires a new rule for the transformation of space and time.

This rule is precisely the **Lorentz transformation.** We will unfold it in detail in the next chapter.

---

## 10. Three Pillars and One Unresolved Question

At this point, the three physical pillars of the GULP framework are all in place. Together, they answer three foundational questions:

Conservation of momentum answers: what remains invariant during the process of change?

Invariance of the speed of light answers: how does the information of change propagate?

The principle of relativity answers: how do different observers share the same universe?

But there remains one final question: why does the universe change? What mechanism drives change to occur? Conservation of momentum describes constraints; invariance of the speed of light describes the upper limit of propagation; the principle of relativity describes the universality of laws—none of the three tells us where the engine of change lies. The pursuit of evolutionary dynamics remains an open question hanging over the GULP framework. The answer to this question does not lie in the first three chapters; it requires us to continue searching in deeper physical structures.
