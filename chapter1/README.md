### 2. The Zero-Bug Degree

The meaning of the Zero-Bug Degree is simple: whether a system, amid change, can maintain its own structure and coherence. If it can, it continues to exist. If it cannot, it disintegrates. It is not about being flawless, nor about being static and unchanging. It refers to dynamic stability—under continuous disturbance, the system maintains its continuity through internal regulation.

So, what laws in physics fit this description? What law ensures that a system does not spontaneously collapse amid change?

If the universe itself is also an evolving system, then the first question is not how it changes, but whether it allows change to happen at all. A system capable of sustained evolution must first possess a stable structure of constraints.

From this perspective, the law of conservation of momentum is one fundamental manifestation of the Zero-Bug Degree in the physical world—it describes how a system, in the process of change, still retains certain indestructible invariants.

### 3. The Law of Conservation of Momentum

The law of conservation of momentum is stated as follows: when a system experiences no external force, or the sum of external forces acting on it is zero, the total momentum of the system remains constant.

Its formula is simple:

> p = mv

p is momentum, m is mass, and v is velocity. Momentum is a vector, possessing both magnitude and direction. For a closed system, the total momentum before a collision equals the total momentum after the collision:

> m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = m₁v₁' + m₂v₂'

For an isolated system, regardless of what happens internally—collisions, explosions, reorganizations—the total momentum remains constant. The system can undergo myriad changes, but the changes are constrained within an invariant framework. This is the most fundamental physical embodiment of dynamic stability.

This law did not come from thin air. Descartes first proposed the concept of the conservation of quantity of motion. Huygens discovered that momentum must account for direction. Newton's Third Law—action and reaction are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction—directly implies conservation of momentum. When two objects collide, A exerts a force on B, and B exerts a force of equal magnitude but opposite direction on A. With the interaction time being the same, the momentum lost by A is exactly equal to the momentum gained by B. The total momentum remains unchanged.

### 4. Noether's Theorem

A deeper understanding of momentum conservation comes from Noether's Theorem. In 1918, Emmy Noether proved a profound mathematical theorem: every continuous symmetry corresponds to a conservation law.

Simply put, if the universe retains some hidden invariance before and after a certain transformation, a corresponding conserved quantity emerges. Spatial translation symmetry—the laws of physics are exactly the same here and there—corresponds to conservation of momentum. Time translation symmetry—the laws of physics are exactly the same today and tomorrow—corresponds to conservation of energy.

This means that conservation of momentum is not an empirical regularity stumbled upon through experiments, but a logical necessity arising from the symmetry of spacetime. Within the currently known framework of physics, as long as spatial translation symmetry holds, conservation of momentum is an inevitable result.

Noether's Theorem requires the symmetry to be continuous. Spatial translation can take arbitrarily small values. The partial derivatives of the Lagrangian with respect to coordinates must exist. Only then can the Euler-Lagrange equations hold, and the derivation of conserved quantities be completed. Continuity, integrability, and differentiability—these three together constitute the mathematical foundation of Noether's Theorem.

### 5. Conservation of Momentum and the Evolutionary Curve

This also provides a physical constraint for the continuity of the evolutionary curve.

Within the framework of continuous spacetime and continuous dynamics, conservation of momentum requires that changes in a system's state must be accomplished through continuous processes. When a system evolves from one state to the next, there can be no uncaused jumps in between.

If the evolutionary curve exhibited a jump—the system leaping directly from t₁ to t₂, skipping the intermediate states—then how did p₁ become p₂? In that missing stretch of time, what interactions produced how much impulse? All of it would be a blank. For continuous dynamical processes at the classical scale, states cannot jump without cause.

One clarification is needed here: the above discussion pertains to continuous dynamical processes at the classical scale. At the quantum scale, the evolution of states follows the laws of quantum mechanics. Discrete transitions do not imply a violation of conservation laws, because the transition process is still constrained by the conservation of energy, momentum, and other conserved quantities.

### 6. Conservation of Momentum and the Zero-Bug Degree

At this point, the law of conservation of momentum has provided solid physical support for GULP's Zero-Bug Degree.

The Zero-Bug Degree requires that a system does not collapse amid change, and what conservation of momentum guarantees is that there exist indestructible constraints during the system's evolutionary process, not that specific structures remain forever unchanged. A star can explode, its structure can completely disintegrate, but throughout this process, the total momentum remains a constant. The constraint persists; the structure can change. The Zero-Bug Degree is not philosophical fantasy; it finds its physical counterpart in conservation of momentum.

At the same time, conservation of momentum also corroborates the continuity of the evolutionary curve—within the framework of continuous spacetime and continuous dynamics, changes of state must be accomplished through continuous processes. One single law simultaneously provides a physical foundation for two of GULP's core inquiries.

### 7. Evolutionary Dynamics: Unresolved Questions

But one problem remains unresolved.

Conservation of momentum tells us that the precondition for a system's momentum to remain unchanged is the absence of external forces. Conversely, if the momentum has changed, then some external factor must have intervened. By how much it changed, one can infer the magnitude and direction of the acting force. Where is the engine? Why does it accelerate? Why does it decelerate? Every step must have an answer.

This is evolutionary dynamics. What it pursues is not "what remains unchanged," but "what drives the change."

Returning to GULP's questioning of the Big Bang Theory. The Big Bang Theory describes the evolutionary journey of the universe from the very early era to today, but it does not fully answer: Why did inflation begin? Why did inflation end? Who or what drove the acceleration and deceleration of spatial expansion? Evolutionary dynamics demands that every step of change has a traceable cause, yet our current understanding of these driving forces still contains gaps.

Conservation of momentum provides a starting point for evolutionary dynamics—change must have a source. But it does not tell us what that source is, or how it propagates. How does a localized event affect other parts of the system? Does it arrive instantaneously, or does it propagate at a finite speed?

The answers to these questions do not lie within conservation of momentum. They require another law to answer.
